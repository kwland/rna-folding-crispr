05

The study

Four tests of
the same idea

A flat correlation is easy to dismiss. Chapter 04 measured seed openness one way, on one molecule, at one assumed position, and found nothing. Four objections to that result are answered below, each with a different measurement, and both screens are analyzed the same way so that a result appearing in only one of them is visible as such.

Experiment 01

Five ways to measure accessibility

loading

Counting unpaired bases in one predicted structure gives a value that can only be a multiple of one eighth. Across 4,685 guides that produces 9 distinct numbers. The partition function produces 4,668. The table reports the correlation with measured editing efficiency under both instruments, folded both as a bare spacer and as the full sgRNA.

Two correlations are given for each measure. The pooled value compares every guide against every other, including guides targeting different genes. The within-gene value compares guides only against others aimed at the same gene, which is the comparison a designer makes. Genes differ widely in how editable they are, so the two can disagree sharply.

Experiment 02

Every position, not an assumed window

The seed is conventionally the last eight bases. That is an assumption rather than a measurement. Per-base unpaired probabilities allow the correlation with efficiency to be computed separately at each of the 20 spacer positions, so the assumption can be tested instead of imposed. Experiment 03

Whether structure adds anything

Whether a feature correlates with activity on its own is the wrong question, because sequence composition already predicts a great deal. The useful question is whether folding adds anything on top. A sequence-only model using position-specific bases, neighbouring pairs, and G/C content sets the baseline. Folding features are then added and the change in accuracy is measured on guides the model never saw, holding out whole genes at a time.

Experiment 04

Whether it holds in another laboratory

A result that appears in only the screen it was found in is not a result. Each model is trained on one screen and applied unchanged to another. The two Doench screens share no genes, and CRISPRscan differs in organism, delivery, and readout, so both directions are genuine tests rather than reshuffles of one experiment.

Summary

What the four experiments settle

Measurement

The sharper instrument recovers slightly more than the coarse one, in the direction the hypothesis predicts, but the largest value found anywhere accounts for under half a percent of the variation in activity.

Context

Folding a spacer alone and folding it inside the sgRNA give different answers, correlated at only 0.17. A bare 20-letter spacer has almost no structure to speak of, so the original premise rested on a quantity that barely varies.

Position

The two screens place their strongest positions at opposite ends of the spacer. Effects localized to different places in different experiments are not describing one mechanism.

Value

In human cells folding adds nothing to a sequence-only model. In zebrafish it adds a small amount, in the direction opposite to the hypothesis, and that gain does not transfer.

07

Learn

RNA folding,
explained

A short introduction to the biology, the algorithms, and the site.

1

The molecule

RNA is a sequence built from four bases: A, C, G, and U.

Complementary bases can pair, causing the strand to form stems, loops, and other structures.

2

Base pairing

A pairs with U, G pairs with C, and G can also form a weaker wobble pair with U.

Pairing between distant positions brings the strand together. The models predict this secondary structure: which bases pair, rather than the full molecular shape.

3

Two models, two objectives

A sequence can support many possible folds. The two models choose among them differently:

  • Nussinov: maximizes the number of allowed base pairs.
  • Zuker-style: searches for a lower-energy structure.
4

The value of two models

The models make different assumptions, so comparing them shows when a result depends on the scoring method.

Agreement can be a useful consistency check, but it is not proof of the true structure. The reference section also compares selected outputs with known structures and ViennaRNA results.

5

How this connects to CRISPR

CRISPR uses a short piece of RNA, the guide, to find one exact spot in a whole genome of DNA.

That guide has to be free to stick to the DNA. The tip that touches first is the seed.

The motivating idea was that folding might hide the seed and weaken targeting. The larger dataset on this page does not show that relationship when seed openness is used alone, so the feature should not be used as a standalone filter.

6

What this model predicts

This project predicts RNA secondary structure, not an atom-level 3D structure.

Secondary structure records which bases pair. Dynamic programming can compute that representation quickly for the sequence lengths used here.

The 3D viewer simply lays out the predicted base pairs in space. It should be read as a diagram, not as a molecular structure prediction.

7

What each chapter covers

  • Fold Lab: any strand folded by both models side by side.
  • Analyzer: one guide, with the seed measured as a single structure and across the ensemble.
  • Designer: target DNA turned into a ranked list of guide candidates.

  • Check: the models measured against selected reference structures.
08

How it works

How the
models work

Both methods use dynamic programming: solve smaller intervals, store those results, and combine them to solve the full sequence. Their main difference is how they score a structure.

Model 1

Nussinov, 1978

Goal: the most base pairs

N[i,j] = max N[i+1, j] N[i, j-1] N[i+1, j-1] + pair(i,j) maxk N[i,k] + N[k+1,j]
  • Every allowed pair is worth exactly +1
  • Runs in O(n3) time and is easy to trace by hand
  • Ignores physics, so a stack and a lone pair count the same
  • Over pairs in practice, inventing pairs that would not hold
Model 2

Zuker, 1981

Goal: the lowest free energy

V[i,j] = min eH(i,j) hairpin eS(i,j) + V[i+1,j-1] stack eL(i,j,k,l) + V[k,l] bulge / internal WM[i+1,k] + WM[k+1,j-1] + a multiloop
  • Stacking holds the strand together, loops cost energy
  • Uses nearest neighbour energies measured in a lab
  • Lone pairs get penalised away on their own
  • This is the idea behind ViennaRNA and RNAfold

Energy model

Nearest-neighbor stacking energies

These values describe the energy of stacking one base pair above another, in kcal/mol at 37 °C. More negative values are more stabilizing. G/C-rich stacks are often among the strongest.

These ten values come from published Turner and Xia nearest-neighbor parameters. The G·U wobble, loop, and multiloop terms in this project are simplified. Treat the reported energies as approximate rankings; ViennaRNA remains the reference implementation.

Main results and limitations

What worked

  • Both recurrences are implemented from scratch. On GGGAAACCC the Zuker-style model returns −1.12 kcal/mol, the same value obtained by working the stacking table through by hand.
  • On three selected reference shapes, the Zuker-style model reaches F1 = 1.00, including the tRNA cloverleaf. Nussinov misses that branched structure, as a pair-counting model can.
  • Against precomputed ViennaRNA results for 30 guides, the energy ranking correlates at about r = 0.87, while the absolute values remain systematically different.

Important limits

  • Seed openness does not predict editing efficiency in the pooled dataset (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.01). Chapters 04 and 05 show the result.
  • The reported energies do not match ViennaRNA exactly because the loop and wobble terms are simplified. They are systematically less negative.
  • Real editing also depends on chromatin, repair, target context, off-target effects, and Cas9 behavior. None of those factors is modeled here.
  • The models exclude pseudoknots, folding kinetics, alternative conformations, tertiary interactions, and RNA-protein binding. The 3D view is a schematic, not a molecular model.
09

Project background

About the
project

Linus Tan built the folding algorithms and this interface in order to follow every step from the recurrence to the result, then tested the idea against published guide-activity measurements to establish where it holds up and where it does not.

What this project contains

  • Folding algorithms · Nussinov base-pair maximization, Zuker minimum free energy, and the McCaskill partition function, in both Python and JavaScript.
  • Ensemble accessibility · base-pair probabilities from the inside and outside recursions, checked against exhaustive enumeration of every structure for short sequences.
  • The study · folding features for 5,705 measured guides across two screens, a sequence-only baseline, and cross-validation that holds out whole genes.
  • This site · the guide tools, arc diagrams, dynamic-programming grids, 3D view, and accuracy scoring.

Where the data comes from

Guide activity is from Doench et al. 2014 and 2016, pooled through CRISPOR, and from CRISPRscan (Moreno-Mateos et al. 2015) for the zebrafish replication. Every accessibility value, energy, and model weight on this page is computed by this project's own code. The ViennaRNA comparison values in chapter 06 are output of the ViennaRNA package, precomputed because it does not run in a browser.
